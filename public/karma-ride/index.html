Karma Ride

Fair allocation of city-center access to ride-hailing drivers. Without money.

We manage downtown ride-hailing with a cordon: during rush hours, only a fixed number of permitted vehicles may serve the city center. The permits are allocated not by money and not by luck, but with karma, a non-tradable credit that lets every driver express how much tomorrow's access matters to them.

01 · Motivation

Drivers gravitate toward the center

Ride-hailing drivers go where the money is, and in most cities that is downtown: requests are dense, trips chain quickly, and empty kilometers are short. Each driver's choice is individually rational. Collectively, it breaks the city in two ways at once.

Inside the center: too many

Idle vehicles pile up exactly where streets are scarcest. Cruising supply adds congestion, slows everyone down, and erodes the very earnings that attracted it.

In the periphery: too few

Suburban passengers face the longest waits and the most abandoned requests, precisely at peak hours when they need service most.

The imbalance is not a market failure of demand. It is a spatial allocation problem on the supply side, and it invites a policy that reshapes where drivers are allowed to compete.

02 · The policy

A cordon for ride-hailing

We propose a cordon area around the center: during rush hours, only vehicles holding a permit may serve trips that touch the cordon. The number of permits is fixed at a quota N, chosen by the city.

Calmer center

Fewer taxis compete downtown, so congestion drops and permitted vehicles run faster.

Served periphery

Unpermitted supply relocates outward, and underserved regions finally get picked up.

Shaped demand

Center-to-center trips become scarcer by design, nudging short downtown rides toward public transportation: greener and more space-efficient.

The cordon works. But it creates a new object: the permit, a scarce resource that must be allocated to drivers every day. And how it is allocated decides whether the policy is fair.

03 · Allocation

Who gets a permit?

Money?

Auctioning permits for francs favors drivers with greater ability to pay, not those who need the access most. Outside wealth buys priority.

Lottery?

Fair ex ante, but only ex ante. Repeated bad luck is possible, and the lottery is deaf to who actually needs the permit today.

Rotation?

Everyone gets a turn, but on a schedule that ignores schedules: your assigned day may be exactly the day you cannot drive.

What we want is a mechanism that is fair over time, immune to outside wealth, and still responsive to daily needs. That is what karma is for.

Karma: an artificial currency

Every registered driver holds a balance of karma points (KP): non-tradable credits that are worthless outside the mechanism and cannot be bought with money. Each evening, drivers bid karma for tomorrow's permits.

STEP 1

Bid

Each driver submits a karma bid for tomorrow, up to their balance. Not driving? Bid zero.

STEP 2

Allocate

The highest N bidders win the N permits.

STEP 3

Pay

Winners all pay the same price: the lowest winning bid.

STEP 4

Redistribute

Collected karma is returned equally to all drivers. Nothing enters, nothing leaves.

Karma is conserved and budget-balanced by design. Yielding today literally becomes priority tomorrow.

Three drivers, three evenings

The same rule reads differently depending on the day you are having. That is the point.

🧒The school run
"Tomorrow I pick up my kid right in the rush hour, so I can't drive anyway. I'm not bidding tonight. The karma I save is my priority for next week."
→ bids 0, saves balance
🏠Rent is due
"My rent is due soon and I need the money. Tomorrow I want the high-gain hours downtown, so tonight I bid high."
→ bids high, urgent need
⚖️The recent winner
"I've been bidding high lately and won quite a few permits. Now my balance is running low, I can't bid high again this week... but that's fair. Nobody should get the resource every single day."
→ must yield, fairness enforced

Karma turns private schedules and private urgency into honest daily priorities, without ever touching anyone's wallet.

04 · Next step

Why a daily-basis simulation

Our poster results evaluate the evening peak in isolation: one rush hour, repeated. But karma's whole point is intertemporal. Today's bid drains tomorrow's balance; a permit changes not just where you drive but whether and how long you work; and a parent's Tuesday is not a moonlighter's Tuesday.

To see what cordon + karma really does, we need the full day and the full week: how heterogeneous drivers bid, and how the policy reshapes their daily work plans. So we are building a daily-basis simulation, one simulated day = 1,440 one-minute steps of requests, dispatch, congestion, fares, and operating costs, producing each driver's net income.

Inside the simulation

Demand. A monocentric synthetic city with an hourly demand profile: request origins concentrate inside the cordon, destinations spread across the whole map.

Personas. Each driver has a fixed persona and a moving daily state. The persona is summarized by a target daily income T (the reference point the driver tries to reach, missing it hurts about twice as much as exceeding it helps), an hourly reservation wage r(h) (what an hour of their time is worth outside driving), and an availability window. Daily shocks (a sick child, a due bill) move the daily state through a Markov chain.

The permit is worth most where the feasible window overlaps the peak: the moonlighter can only work the cordon hours, so access is everything to them.

Two decisions, one policy

D1

Work tomorrow? How long, and when?

Choose a work plan: a set of hours (possibly none), weighing expected earnings against the reservation wage and the income target.

D2

How much karma to bid?

Choose tonight's bid for tomorrow's permit, given the current balance and the day's state.

These two decisions cannot be separated. A permit pays only through the hours you actually work, so the bid is worth nothing without the plan. And the best plan depends on whether you hold a permit: a holder leans into the peak inside the cordon, a non-holder shifts hours outward or skips the day. Both choices are driven by the same daily state (availability, urgency, target vs. reservation wage) and linked across days by the karma balance: karma spent today is bidding power gone tomorrow. We therefore solve for them jointly, as one coupled policy.
05 · Method

Seeking the equilibrium

No driver decides in a vacuum. If everyone floods the evening peak, peak earnings collapse and the smart move is to work the morning instead. The model is only coherent at a fixed point: a population of work plans and bids under which no driver wants to change their own plan or bid.

Simulating a full day for every candidate policy would be hopeless, so we split the problem:

SIMULATE ONCERun the day simulator on a space-filling set of supply mixtures, offline.
IDENTIFYFit an hourly earnings map: how the net hourly rate falls as supply rises, by permit state.
ITERATESolve the fixed point on the identified map with damped logit responses, in milliseconds.
VERIFYRerun the true simulator at the solution and certify that it reproduces the prediction.

In the loop, z is the daily state, k the karma balance, p the permit state, a the work plan and b the bid. Policies generate hourly supply; the identified map translates supply into hourly earnings; plan utilities aggregate earnings against reservation wages and the income target; a smoothed (logit) best response updates the plan policy and then the bidding policy. The iteration is damped, started from several initial mixtures, and its fixed point is certified by a full simulator rerun. The unrestricted and lottery variants of this pipeline are already running and validated; the karma-coupled version is the step we are building now.
